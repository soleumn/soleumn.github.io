<br>The chat flew by so fast it was hard to read.

The Young god watched the screen in silence, smiling.

As viewers clamored to become his sponsor, he said nothing.

They argued among themselves, then even came up with a set of rules.

MTube offered a donation feature that let viewers send money to the M-tubers hosting broadcasts.

The rule they settled on was that whoever sent the most Star Fragments through donations would become his official sponsor.

The Young god watched the people fighting in the chat as if he found it amusing.

He enjoyed watching them struggle to reach him, but then he abruptly froze.

“…Ah.”

The Young god let out a brief groan, then spoke in a much calmer tone.

“That was… a greeting to celebrate the start of the broadcast.”

He seemed like an entirely different person from before—as if he’d become another god.

Unusually composed for someone hosting his first broadcast, the Young god continued.

“First, thank you for coming to watch. As I said earlier, I’m looking for someone to sign an official sponsorship contract with me.”

All kinds of comments poured into the chat.

It was a moment when countless people focused their attention on a single person all at once.

He had every reason to be flustered, but the Young god read through the comments one by one and answered them.

“I don’t plan on opening donations just yet. Today’s only a temporary broadcast, after all. And…”

Ever since the stream began, several people had been spamming the chat, insisting that his golden eyes were fake.

“I see some of you are asking if I’m a fake.”

The Young god paused. The mood shifted in an instant once again.

After watching the screen quietly, he suddenly leaned close to the camera.

The chat slowed for a moment as his golden eyes filled the screen.

The Young god tilted his head back and forth, muttering to himself.

“Can you see them better like this?”

He swept his bangs aside with a hand, showing off his golden eyes from several angles.

Then he raised one corner of his mouth and asked,

“Do I still look fake?”

The people who’d been making a fuss about him being a fake fell completely silent.

The Young god leaned back from the camera. Then, suddenly, he dragged a hand down his face.

After that slightly weary gesture, he continued in an even tone.

“…As you can see, I’m the real deal. But if you still think I’m fake, there’s not much I can do about it.”

The Young god went on to read and answer a few more comments.

“My home planet’s coordinates? That’s a secret. You’re offering me a galaxy as a gift? Then hand it over right now… Ah, no. That was a slip of the tongue. It’s the thought that counts.”

“A lot of you are asking why I like my subordinates so much. Isn’t it strange not to like them? And don’t go coveting my subordinates! No, I’d appreciate it if you didn’t. I’ve never thought of them as toys.”

The chat flew by too quickly to follow, but the Young god picked out the questions viewers cared about and answered them with ease.

He seemed remarkably accustomed to dealing with an audience.

At some point, the chat began to move in time with the Young god’s words.

Then his gaze turned toward something offscreen.

As if someone were there with him, he exchanged a few glances with them, then announced that the broadcast was ending.

“That’s all for today.”

Questions about sponsorship contracts flooded the chat.

The Young god skimmed the comments and continued.

“I’ll explain the sponsorship contract in detail during my next broadcast. Today, I just wanted to see if there was anyone who might want to become my sponsor. So…”

The Young god paused for a moment, then grinned.

“Come watch my next broadcast, too.”

With that, the screen went black.

***

<br>

Once the broadcast ended, I checked to make sure it was over and let out a small breath.

The Young god had popped up here and there, which had been a bit of a problem, but it seemed to have gone well enough.

It felt like the viewers had actually enjoyed it more when I acted all jumbled up.

Mo Haein, who’d been watching beside me, asked with wide eyes,

“Have you streamed before?”

She said I’d seemed really good at it, and I answered with a slightly sheepish smile.

“I did it for a little while back when I was a Player.”

The Player contract had included a clause requiring me to livestream for a certain number of hours each month.

It was something I had to do, but I’d actually enjoyed streaming quite a bit.

It gave me time to play all sorts of games and interact with my fans.

There were some people who said strange things, but the kind words from my fans meant much more to me.

Even plenty of people who weren’t my fans came to watch me play through games, so my streams always had a fairly decent number of viewers.

Thanks to that experience, dealing with the aliens hadn’t been particularly difficult.

The chat had been more or less fine, too. There had been one comment, though, that stuck in my mind.

I recalled a comment that had flashed by.

<br>

<span style="color: var(--texto-secundario);">— Turn off the stream right now.</span>

<br>

It stood out among all the aliens who said whatever they wanted.

Even the aliens who sent hostile comments hadn’t told me to turn off the stream.

They were all worked up over the exciting spectacle, so I couldn’t help noticing the one person taking a different approach.

What kind of alien was it?

How dare they give me orders? The nerve of that… alien—

No, not an alien…

I decided I’d check whether they sent something similar during the next broadcast.

Anyway, the only reason I’d been able to stream this time was thanks to the Administrator.

*‘Thank you, Administrator.’*

I’d hurriedly learned how to use Akasha’s MTube from him, then started the broadcast.

Once I’d learned how to create a channel, I was able to figure out the rest myself, since it was very similar to Earth’s MTube.

Thanks to that, I’d come up with a new theory.

The Creator’s broadcast I’d watched in the past had probably been…

*‘Akasha’s MTube.’*

That would explain why Samra hadn’t properly recognized the Creator’s broadcast.

If it had taken place in Akasha, then hardly any traces of it would have remained on Earth.

But there was still something that didn’t add up.

The System Window that had appeared when I reached 10% recognition meant that Earth hadn’t belonged to Akasha before that point.

If so, we shouldn’t have been able to use Akasha’s civilization, either. How had the Creator used Akasha’s MTube to broadcast?

*I didn’t think the Creator was some being from beyond Earth.*

After all, he’d gathered 666 people to make them play a game, then turned me into Earth’s god.

As I was lost in thought, Kwak Hanmuk tidied up the camera and asked,

“You’re not actually going to sign a sponsorship contract, right?”

He’d watched the broadcast with Mo Haein and helped set up the equipment.

“No. I’m not.”

I’d made a major announcement about looking for a sponsor, but I had no intention of actually signing a sponsorship contract.

I wasn’t reckless enough to do something like that without Dominic’s permission.

So why had I pulled that kind of publicity stunt?

The sponsorship contract was bait to lure out Black Block.

*‘It’s what Black Block wants most.’*

From the beginning, he’d wanted the position of the Young god’s sponsor.

If I looked as though I might sign a sponsorship contract with another alien, he wouldn’t be able to stand by and would come out of hiding.

*‘It’d be best if an alien as powerful as Black Block made a move.’*

In this vast universe, there had to be someone Black Block would be wary of.

It’d be nice if Dominic took the bait, too, but I doubted this would be enough to get him moving.

Either way, I’d cast my line. All that was left was to wait.

I was wondering when Black Block might react when I spoke up.

“Um, and Captains.”

I hesitated a little before continuing.

“Please don’t worry about the toy comment. They’re aliens, so I think their ideas are a little different from ours.”

They’d already have a hard time understanding things like gods and subordinates. Then the aliens had started appearing one after another.

Captain Kwak and Captain Mo were probably confused in all sorts of ways.

They’d even heard an alien talk about treating subordinates like toys.

I made sure to reassure my subordinates so they wouldn’t feel uneasy.

“I’ll protect you, so don’t worry—no, please don’t worry.”

As I explained that people from Earth didn’t have to follow alien customs, Kwak Hanmuk folded his arms and said,

“Are you okay?”

“Huh?”

“You’re in more danger now.”

I didn’t immediately understand what he meant by that, but then it dawned on me.

He was talking about the fanatical attention directed at me.

I’d told the Captains everything about Black Block, too, so I’d probably given them even more reason to worry.

When I tried to explain that we were safer now, Kwak Hanmuk furrowed his brow and asked,

“Is there anything else you haven’t told us?”

“…No, nothing.”

I felt a little guilty, but I said there wasn’t.

I couldn’t help it.

I’d told them about the Epic Quest and honestly shared a great many other things with them.

But there was one thing I hadn’t been able to tell them.

Dominic.

There was no point in telling them about an unavoidable catastrophe if it would only make them more anxious.

Mo Haein, who’d been quietly listening to us talk, asked,

“The god in the Tower is very different from the Trainee, right? In the way he thinks and all that.”

“He’s as different from us as aliens are from people on Earth.”

Kwak Hanmuk answered for me, just as the shelter’s old door swung open with a loud clatter.

The person ducking her head to avoid hitting the doorframe as she entered was Cecilia.

When she returned to the shelter, the first person she looked at was me.

Then she glanced at Kwak Hanmuk and Mo Haein before turning her gaze back to me.

“……”

Silence followed.

I looked at her with my eyes still golden and suddenly thought how lucky it was that I’d only become half Young god.

If the Young god had taken control, he might have been cruel to her, caring as he did only for his subordinates.

Of course, I was still putting my subordinates first to some extent.

I couldn’t help it when my subordinates were so cute and precious.

Still, in my current state, I could think about Cecilia without being completely distracted by my subordinates.

After holding her gaze for a long moment, I called out to her.

“Noona.”

The moment I did, clear relief appeared on Cecilia’s face.

I realized then that she’d been worried about the same thing I had.

That I might become a complete god and look at her as if she were an insignificant creature.

I slowly opened my mouth and said,

“I’m going up the Tower.”

“…Roasted Chestnut.”

“I’m sorry. But I have to go up.”

I remembered the promise I’d made to Cecilia before.

> “Give up on the Tower. And never come back to Hyper Eden.”

Every Stringer dreamed of climbing the Tower.

They wanted to uncover secrets unknown to anyone in Hyper Eden, and many Stringers had tried.

I was no different.

When I played the game, I’d wanted to climb the Tower to learn the truth about the city.

Cecilia had helped me, and just before the True Ending, I’d found a way to sneak into the Tower.

Or at least, I’d thought I had.

The moment I’d tried to sneak inside the Tower came back to me with perfect clarity.

Remembering what had happened then, I said,

“It’ll be okay this time.”

This time, I’d make Black Block, the Tower’s master, open the door himself. Surely things would be different from before.

But Cecilia wouldn’t let me go.

“…Then we’ll go up together.”

She glared at me with fierce eyes.

“I’m not letting you go alone.”

I was just thinking that I wasn’t alone when—

“Noona, what are you so worried about?”

Kwak Hanmuk slung an arm around my shoulders with a thump. Then he gave Cecilia a crooked smile.

“You’ve got two younger siblings here.”

He was faithfully sticking to his foster-sibling story.

I suppressed the urge to blush with embarrassment.

What surprised me a little was that Kwak Hanmuk wasn’t the only one to step forward.

Mo Haein silently came over and stood beside me, too.

If she were only playing along with the lie, there was no need to go this far.

I thought it was a little unlike Mo Haein, but Cecilia gave a short, mirthless laugh.

She murmured slowly,

“Right. Younger siblings…”

As I briefly wondered whether taking foster siblings as subordinates was strange by alien standards, Cecilia gazed steadily at the Captains and curled her lips into a smile.

Her eyes narrowed as she said,

“Let’s all go up there together.”

Only later did I realize that it had been a declaration of war.

<br>

While we waited for Black Block to open the Tower’s door, we had to rush through the quests as quickly as possible to close the Trial.

So I came up with a plan for a double life: Young god streams during the day, Stringer work at night.

Day News hadn’t revealed Earth’s coordinates, and they’d edited out the footage of me using my divine powers during the live broadcast.

They probably wouldn’t interfere if I put on a mask and worked as a Stringer at night.

If anything, I was sure Black Block would want to watch how I cleared the quests.

I’d failed the previous quest to film a monster at a fast-food restaurant because I’d been dragged off to an arcade.

I’d missed out on a story that could have earned me a fortune.

Still, the monster’s sound that I’d sold to the radio show’s &lt;Spooky Sound> segment had brought in a decent amount, so I’d managed to scrape together enough money to buy all the basic filming equipment.

A slate, a boom mic, one light, and a camera.

I’d handle filming with the camera, Mo Haein would hold the slate, Kwak Hanmuk would take the boom mic, and…

Cecilia would handle the light.

No matter how many times I tried to dissuade her, Cecilia insisted on coming along to clear the quest.

I had no choice but to tell her about my half-Young-god state and warn her in advance that I might do something strange from time to time.

There were quests I’d done with Cecilia in the game, but she’d never followed me from the very beginning like this.

I awkwardly adjusted the white half-mask covering my face, then looked ahead.

The convenience store was closed and all its lights were off, but a bluish glow still shone from the refrigerated display cases.

The Captains and Cecilia stood in front of a section displaying synthetic convenience foods, like high-calorie jelly tubes and concentrated caffeine capsules.

Cecilia’s help made things remarkably convenient.

She started by tampering with the store’s basic AI checkout kiosk.

The kiosk, which had been about to detect our nighttime break-in and sound the alarm, crackled and began playing its customer greeting.

<br>

*“Welcome, Labor Grade E customer. High-calorie protein blocks are on a buy-two-get-one-free special. 100% cultured-meat hot bars are 50% off.”*

<br>

The kiosk’s greeting changed depending on your grade, and the higher a Stringer’s reputation, the higher their Labor Grade.

As a new Stringer, my Labor Grade was still low.

I turned on the camera, checked the screen, and said,

“My precious subordinate, come over here… No, sigh. Captain Mo, could you stand in front of this for a moment?”

Mo Haein took the slate and stood in front of it.

As I tested the shot, Kwak Hanmuk yawned while holding out the boom mic.

“Chestnut, when does the monster show up?”

“If we start searching now, it’ll probably take about an hour.”

A Shadow Man was hiding in the gaps between the shelves of the mega convenience store.

A black figure with only eyes embedded in its form, the Shadow Man lured in and killed people who passed near the store after closing.

It especially targeted people with high Labor Grades, making it a real nuisance.

A famous figure in Hyper Eden had once fallen victim to a Shadow Man, so public interest in them was high.

If I filmed it well and sold the footage, it would get a great response…

“…Then the more monsters there are, the better.”

“Huh?”

The boom mic in Kwak Hanmuk’s hands slipped. I looked at him and pressed the record button on the camera.

“Look over here.”

I filmed my subordinates with the camera.

After capturing the thing I most wanted to film, I gave an order.

“…!”

The moment I’d done it, I came back to my senses. But it was already too late.

Cecilia quickly drew her laser gun. Mo Haein tossed aside the slate and drew the Black Moon Sword.

*Bzzzt, bzzzt.*

A harsh static noise rang out, and one of the ceiling lights, which had been off, flickered on with a pop.

Then one after another, the lights switched on with a rapid series of clicks, and the whole store lit up.

As the lights turned on and off erratically, the long shadows stretching between the shelves bent and twisted of their own accord.

The moment the lights came on, the space was empty. But during each brief blackout, it seemed to fill to the brim with something before emptying again.

The steadily running refrigerator cut out, replaced by a noise like metal scraping against metal.

Damp footsteps followed, like someone walking barefoot through mud.

*Sssss…*

Thick, viscous black sludge bubbled up through the gaps in the urethane tiles beneath the shelves.

Tendrils burst through the walls with a cracking sound, while something like sticky hair poured endlessly from the gaps in the pipes overhead.

<br>

*“Y-Young god, c-customer! N-night shift is n-not a-allowed, kid, go to bed!”*

<br>

The kiosk spat out broken mechanical words.

*Dududududu-!*

Everything began to vibrate.

*Crash!*

Every window in the store shattered at once, and black shapes poured in from outside like a wave.

The Young god had summoned every monster in Hyper Eden to the convenience store.

In the unprecedented chaos, I hurried everyone up onto the shelves first.

“Captains! I’ll explain later! We have to film this first!”

Then I raised the camera and shouted desperately,

“This is all money!”
